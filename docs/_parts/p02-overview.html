The wedge, the shape, and the money

Mobile Dinners is three products that only work because they are one product: an operating system for the restaurant, a growth engine that runs on its data, and a consumer marketplace that monetizes the network the first two create.

The problem, stated precisely

An independent restaurant doing $1.2M/yr in revenue runs on six to nine disconnected systems: a POS (Toast, Square, Clover), a website (Wix or a nephew), a delivery aggregator or three (DoorDash, Uber Eats, Grubhub), an email tool (Mailchimp), a loyalty punch card, a reservation system, and a spreadsheet. Each holds a fragment of the customer. None of them talk. The aggregators hold the most valuable fragment — who ordered what, when, and how often — and rent it back at 15–30% of gross.

The compounding damage is not the fee. It is that the restaurant never accumulates an asset. Ten years of DoorDash volume produces zero owned demand. The day they turn the app off, the demand evaporates, because it was never theirs.

The three planes

Everything in this document belongs to exactly one of three planes. The plane determines its latency budget, its failure mode, and its deployment cadence.

   RULE: Plane A never takes a synchronous dependency on B or C.
   A restaurant with no internet must still take orders, fire tickets,

This is the single most important architectural constraint in the system, and it is the one most commonly violated by cloud-native POS startups. A kitchen at 7:40pm on a Friday cannot wait on a recommendation service. Plane A is a local-first system that replicates to the cloud; it is not a thin client.

Founder note — the actual moat

Neither the POS nor the marketing suite is defensible on its own; both categories have well-funded incumbents. The defensible position is the closed loop: Mobile Dinners is the only party that sees a customer's behavior across many restaurants and owns the menu-of-record and margin data inside each one. That intersection is what lets the system say "promote the $16 bowl to this specific person at 6:10pm on a Thursday because it has 71% margin, 4-minute prep, and she buys it 40% of the time when offered." No aggregator has margin data. No POS has cross-brand behavior.

Engineering principles

Ten rules that resolve most design arguments before they start. Where a later section contradicts one of these, the principle wins and the section is wrong.

  1. Plane A is local-first. The terminal owns a durable local database and an outbox. The cloud is a replica, not the source of truth, during a service window.
  2. The menu is a versioned, immutable artifact. Menus are published as snapshots with content hashes, never mutated in place. Every order references the exact menu version it was placed against.
  3. Money is integer cents in a named currency. No floats anywhere in the pricing, tax, tip, or payout path. Ever.
  4. Every state change is an event. Orders, tickets, payouts, and loyalty balances are projections over an append-only log. Reconstructable history is non-negotiable for accounting and disputes.
  5. Idempotency keys on every mutating endpoint. Restaurant Wi-Fi retries. Assume every write arrives two to five times.
  6. AI proposes, humans dispose — until earned. Every AI action ships first as a suggestion with a one-tap accept. Autonomy is unlocked per-tenant by measured acceptance rate, not by a feature flag someone forgot.
  7. Tenant isolation is enforced at the database, not the ORM. Row-level security with a session-scoped tenant claim. An application bug must not become a cross-tenant data breach.
  8. Never store a PAN. Card data goes device-to-processor. The platform is SAQ-A / P2PE scope, permanently.
  9. Cross-brand loyalty is a liability on a balance sheet. Points are accounted for, escheatment-aware, and breakage-modeled from day one, not bolted on at Series A.
  10. Degrade loudly to operators, silently to guests. A guest should never see a stack trace; an operator should never be surprised by a subsystem that has been down for an hour.

Explicit non-goals

  • Not a full-service table-management / reservations product at V1. Coursing, table splits, and floorplan management are a distinct hard problem; V1 targets quick-service, fast-casual, pizza, and ghost kitchens where ticket flow is linear.
  • Not building an owned delivery fleet at MVP. Dispatch abstracts over third-party courier networks (Nash, Olo Dispatch, DoorDash Drive, Uber Direct) plus restaurant staff drivers. Gig supply is added only in launch metros where density supports it.
  • Not competing on payment processing rate. The platform is a payfac-lite on top of a processor; margin comes from the spread, not from undercutting Stripe.
  • Not an inventory / recipe-costing suite at V1. The optimizer consumes a coarse item-level cost input; deep back-of-house inventory is a V2 module or an integration.
