The wedge, the shape, and the money

Mobile Dinners is three products that only work because they are one product: an operating system for the restaurant, a growth engine that runs on its data, and a consumer marketplace that monetizes the network the first two create.

The problem, stated precisely

An independent restaurant doing $1.2M/yr in revenue runs on six to nine disconnected systems: a POS (Toast, Square, Clover), a website (Wix or a nephew), a delivery aggregator or three (DoorDash, Uber Eats, Grubhub), an email tool (Mailchimp), a loyalty punch card, a reservation system, and a spreadsheet. Each holds a fragment of the customer. None of them talk. The aggregators hold the most valuable fragment — who ordered what, when, and how often — and rent it back at 15–30% of gross.

The compounding damage is not the fee. It is that the restaurant never accumulates an asset. Ten years of DoorDash volume produces zero owned demand. The day they turn the app off, the demand evaporates, because it was never theirs.

Founder note — the actual moat

Neither the POS nor the marketing suite is defensible on its own; both categories have well-funded incumbents. The defensible position is the closed loop: Mobile Dinners is the only party that sees a customer's behavior across many restaurants and owns the menu-of-record and margin data inside each one. That intersection is what lets the system say "promote the $16 bowl to this specific person at 6:10pm on a Thursday because it has 71% margin, 4-minute prep, and she buys it 40% of the time when offered." No aggregator has margin data. No POS has cross-brand behavior.

Engineering principles

Ten rules that resolve most design arguments before they start. Where a later section contradicts one of these, the principle wins and the section is wrong.

  1. Plane A is local-first. The terminal owns a durable local database and an outbox. The cloud is a replica, not the source of truth, during a service window.
  2. The menu is a versioned, immutable artifact. Menus are published as snapshots with content hashes, never mutated in place. Every order references the exact menu version it was placed against.
  3. Money is integer cents in a named currency. No floats anywhere in the pricing, tax, tip, or payout path. Ever.
  4. Every state change is an event. Orders, tickets, payouts, and loyalty balances are projections over an append-only log. Reconstructable history is non-negotiable for accounting and disputes.
  5. Idempotency keys on every mutating endpoint. Restaurant Wi-Fi retries. Assume every write arrives two to five times.
  6. AI proposes, humans dispose — until earned. Every AI action ships first as a suggestion with a one-tap accept. Autonomy is unlocked per-tenant by measured acceptance rate, not by a feature flag someone forgot.
  7. Tenant isolation is enforced at the database, not the ORM. Row-level security with a session-scoped tenant claim. An application bug must not become a cross-tenant data breach.
  8. Never store a PAN. Card data goes device-to-processor. The platform is SAQ-A / P2PE scope, permanently.
  9. Cross-brand loyalty is a liability on a balance sheet. Points are accounted for, escheatment-aware, and breakage-modeled from day one, not bolted on at Series A.
  10. Degrade loudly to operators, silently to guests. A guest should never see a stack trace; an operator should never be surprised by a subsystem that has been down for an hour.

Explicit non-goals

  • Not a full-service table-management / reservations product at V1. Coursing, table splits, and floorplan management are a distinct hard problem; V1 targets quick-service, fast-casual, pizza, and ghost kitchens where ticket flow is linear.
  • Not building an owned delivery fleet at MVP. Dispatch abstracts over third-party courier networks (Nash, Olo Dispatch, DoorDash Drive, Uber Direct) plus restaurant staff drivers. Gig supply is added only in launch metros where density supports it.
  • Not competing on payment processing rate. The platform is a payfac-lite on top of a processor; margin comes from the spread, not from undercutting Stripe.
  • Not an inventory / recipe-costing suite at V1. The optimizer consumes a coarse item-level cost input; deep back-of-house inventory is a V2 module or an integration.

1.3  Event spine

Every meaningful state transition publishes a versioned Avro event to Kafka via the transactional outbox pattern (Debezium CDC on an outbox table inside the same Postgres transaction as the state change). This eliminates the dual-write problem: an order is never committed without its event, and never publishes an event for a rolled-back order.

Why Redpanda over Kafka for the first 18 months

Single binary, no ZooKeeper/KRaft operational surface, Kafka wire-compatible, and roughly a third of the infrastructure cost at low volume. Migrating to MSK or Confluent later is a configuration change, not a rewrite, because the client contract is identical. This is a deliberate trade of ceiling for velocity at a stage where velocity is the constraint.

1.5  Database schema — core tables

Postgres 16 with row-level security. Every tenant-scoped table carries org_id as the first column of its primary key and is covered by an RLS policy keyed to a session GUC set by the connection pool at checkout. Money is BIGINT minor units plus an ISO-4217 code; there is no NUMERIC price column anywhere in the transaction path.

  org_id      uuid NOT NULL,

-- not balance cannot commit. This is the difference between an accounting system and
-- a pile of numbers.
Schema decision — why loyalty is platform-scoped but profiles are tenant-scoped

A guest has one points wallet across every Mobile Dinners restaurant — that is the consumer hook, and it is what an aggregator cannot copy without cannibalizing its own commission. But a restaurant must never be able to query another restaurant's customer relationship. So persons and loyalty_accounts live outside tenant RLS, while customer_profiles is a tenant-scoped projection that exposes only the slice belonging to that org. Cross-brand aggregates reach operators only as anonymized, k-anonymized cohort statistics (minimum cohort size 25).

Risk control — store-and-forward payments

Offline card acceptance transfers chargeback risk to whoever authorizes it. Policy: an offline floor limit (default $75), a per-window aggregate cap (default $2,000), a hard cutoff at 4 hours offline, and a per-tenant opt-in with the risk stated in plain language during onboarding. Above the cap, the terminal requires cash or a signed intent to re-run later. Do not let a growth-hacking instinct set these defaults; let the risk team set them.

Guardrails that ship with V1

  • Claims filter. A deterministic classifier plus a rules list blocks generated text containing allergen assertions ("gluten-free", "nut-free"), health claims, origin claims ("locally sourced"), and superlatives that create liability. Blocked spans are shown to the operator with an explanation, not silently removed.
  • Action bounds. Price changes capped per cycle and per window; discounts cannot push an item below a margin floor; campaign sends capped per guest per week and per org per day; no autonomous action may create financial exposure above a per-tenant ceiling.
  • Autonomy ladder. Every AI capability starts at suggest. It advances to auto-with-undo only after 30 accepted suggestions at ≥80% acceptance for that tenant, and to autonomous only for reversible, bounded actions. The ladder is per-tenant and per-capability, and it can be walked back automatically if acceptance drops.
  • Decision log. Every proposal writes ai.decision.v1 with model version, prompt version, feature snapshot, proposal, human verdict, and the measured 21-day outcome. This is the eval set, the audit trail, and the dispute record simultaneously.
  • Cost governor. Per-tenant and per-task token budgets with hard stops; the router degrades to a cheaper model before it degrades availability, and never silently — the operator sees which tier produced a result.
  • Data boundary. Tenant data is never used to train a model whose outputs are served to a different tenant, except as aggregated statistics with a minimum cohort size of 25. This is a contractual commitment, and it is enforced in the feature pipeline, not in a policy document.

Onboarding rule

Steps 1–9 must never depend on steps 10–14. The load-bearing assumption — read this one twice

Line 3 is where this model lives or dies. A delivery costs $5–$8 to fulfill. If the guest pays $1.99 and the restaurant contributes $2.00, the platform is still short $1–$4 on a marginal delivery. Aggregators close that gap with a 28% commission; Mobile Dinners has given that up by design.

Three levers close it honestly, and the plan must assume all three: (a) batching — at 1.6 orders per courier-trip, effective cost drops to ~$4.30; (b) restaurant-staff drivers during their existing paid shifts, which the operator already funds and which the ladder prefers first; (c) density — below roughly 25 active restaurants per delivery zone, batching does not occur and delivery is structurally unprofitable. Therefore delivery launches per-zone, gated on density, and pickup-first everywhere else. Any plan that launches delivery nationally on day one is a plan to subsidize couriers with equity.

7.5  Growth strategy

Phase 1 — Own one city (months 0–12)

  • Geographic concentration, not national spray. Pick one metro of 400k–900k with a strong independent scene and weak aggregator lock-in. Saturate two or three neighborhoods to Stage 3 density before opening a fourth. A thin national footprint produces a marketplace nobody opens.
  • Sell the arithmetic, not the software. The opening move is a free Commission Audit: connect read-only to their aggregator dashboards, compute exactly what they paid in fees last year, and present it. This is a lead magnet with a hard number on it.
  • Land on Starter, expand to Growth. Coexist mode removes the "I can't change POS mid-season" objection, which kills most restaurant-tech deals in the first meeting.
  • Recruit the anchors first. Ten restaurants with lines out the door create the consumer reason to install. Give them concierge onboarding and, where necessary, a discounted year. Their logos sell the next fifty.

Phase 2 — Consumer flywheel (months 9–24)

  • Acquire consumers at the restaurant, not on Meta. QR table tents, receipt inserts, bag stickers, and a staff-prompted "scan for points" at pickup. CAC here is single-digit dollars against $25–$60 for paid social.
  • The wallet is the retention mechanism. Points that work at 40 local restaurants are strictly better than 40 separate punch cards, and impossible for a single-brand app to match.
  • Deploy restaurant-funded acquisition offers — the restaurant funds a first-order incentive it would otherwise have paid an aggregator 28% for, and it acquires a customer it keeps.

Phase 3 — Multi-market and channel (months 18–36)

  • Replicate with a playbook, three metros at a time, only after unit economics are proven in market one. Each new metro repeats the Stage 1→3 density ladder.
  • Channel partners: restaurant groups, food-hall operators, ghost-kitchen landlords, and local chambers — each brings 5–50 locations per relationship.
  • Franchise and enterprise unlock 10–200 locations per contract at a far lower CAC, but require franchise-tier features (brand governance, royalty reporting, corporate menu control) built in V2.
